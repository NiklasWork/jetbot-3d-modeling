The idea

One robot, one laptop, one folder between them

Left side · the robot

Cover the room and photograph it

  • 1Drive through the room so that every wall gets seen. How it decides where to go is the open question, and slide 7 is about it.
  • 2Stop. Wait 0.7 s until the chassis stops rocking.
  • 3Take one photo. Then drive on.
  • 4Save it as frame_0042.jpg, numbered in the order it was taken.

Photos, not video. A camera in motion smears, and a smeared photo breaks the step that works out where the camera stood.

Right side · the MacBook

Turn the photos into a model

  • 1Fetch the folder off the robot over Wi‑Fi.
  • 2Work out where the camera stood for every single photo.
  • 3Build the 3D model from the photos, visible on screen while it happens.
  • 4Shrink it and write one HTML file that opens the room in any browser.

One word, two things

“Model” means two different things in this project

On the robot · still to be built

The driving model

A camera image goes in, a steering decision comes out, many times a second, on the robot itself. It is trained once and then used everywhere: it works in rooms it has never seen.

What it would have to satisfy:

  • Decide from the camera image alone. The robot has no wheel sensors, no motion sensor and no map of the room.
  • Run on a 2019 Jetson Nano, so it has to be a small network.
  • Be trained on frames we label ourselves, a few hundred of them.

This part does not exist yet. Designing it, collecting the data and training it is the work in front of us.

On the Mac · built and measured

The 3D model of the room

A cloud of hundreds of thousands of small, coloured, see‑through blobs. Each one carries a position, a size, a colour and how solid it is. Nothing else.

Despite the word “training”, nothing here learns. Draw the blobs from a camera position we already know, hold the result next to the real photo, move every blob a little towards the photo. Eight thousand times.

The finished thing decides nothing and predicts nothing. It is the room, and it is useless in any other room.

One of them learns to drive anywhere and knows no room in particular. The other knows one room exactly and nothing else.

Architecture

What happens, in what order, and on which machine

The whole interface is a folder. Numbered JPEG files, no protocol and no shared code, so either side runs and can be repaired without the other. Two different worlds. Python 3.6 frozen on the robot from 2019, Python 3.14 on the Mac. Merged, both sides would compromise for nothing. A third machine, one job. A Lenovo home server hosts the finished viewer so the link survives the talk. It computes nothing.

The driving algorithm · the piece still to be worked out

How does the robot decide where to drive?

1 · written, never run on the robot

A fixed script

The robot knows three figures and drives them blind:

  • Orbit, 12 × a small arc
  • Wall pass, 10 × straight ahead
  • Panorama, 8 × turn on the spot

The human puts the robot down and presses one key. The robot drives the whole figure, stopping for a photo after every step. It senses nothing while it does so.

Two emergency stops: any keypress aborts it, and two identical photos in a row end the figure, which is what a robot pushing against furniture produces.

2 · open

Train a model that runs on the robot

Camera image in, a steering correction out, many times a second, on the robot itself. It would take over the long wall pass, the one figure where driving blind drifts off course.

What it would take:

  • A few hundred photos labelled by hand, one click each: where should it have steered?
  • A small network, ResNet‑18, trained on the robot. Its 2019 software stack is already there, so nothing has to be moved.
  • Hours of exclusive time on a robot that other projects share.
  • A test it can actually fail: steer better than a network that always answers with the average.

Ruled out on purpose: the classic trained obstacle avoider. It learns to drive straight until something blocks it, then turn on the spot. The next slide is why that produces unusable photos.

The rule all three have to satisfy

The camera has to move sideways past what it photographs

To rebuild a surface, the software has to see it from two positions far enough apart. Turning on the spot gives it plenty of overlap and nothing to measure against. Driving straight at a wall gives it nothing either, at least not in the middle of the picture. Passing a wall sideways does. That is the test every one of the three options on the previous slide has to pass, and it is why an obstacle avoider, which only ever drives straight and then turns, delivers photos that cannot be used.

The open problem

The camera sits low, points down, and does not move

If most of every photo is floor, the run produces an excellent floor and a poor room, because a surface that was never photographed cannot be rebuilt. Nobody has measured this. The project's own plan asked for a camera tilted slightly upwards, and the delivered mount does the opposite. One photo taken from the standing robot settles it, and that photo does not exist yet.

What comes out at the end

5.5 MB, loads on click. Drag to look around, scroll to move.

This is not a room our robot drove through. The photos come from a public research dataset (Deep Blending, Dr Johnson, Hedman et al., 2018): 263 photos of a stranger's living room, taken by hand. Everything after that is ours. Our pipeline located all 263 of them, built the model, shrank it and packed it into this page. 159 000 blobs, 5.5 MB, no server involved.
