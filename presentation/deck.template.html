The idea

Five steps, one of which is worth watching

01 Drive

Stop and go

Drive a little, stop, let the chassis settle, take one frame. Repeat. The robot never shoots while moving.

02 Fetch

One command

The Mac pulls the folder off the robot and starts the pipeline in the same breath.

03 Locate

Where was the camera?

COLMAP recovers a position and orientation for every single frame. This is the step that decides everything.

04 Watch

The room assembles

Brush optimises a cloud of translucent ellipsoids until it looks like the photos — and you can fly through it while it runs.

Then a fifth step compresses the result to a fifteenth of its size and writes a viewer you open in a browser. No server needed.

The conceptual trap

“Model” means two different things in this project

The 3D model

No machine learning at all

Despite the word training, nothing learns. Hundreds of thousands of translucent ellipsoids each carry a position, size, orientation, colour and opacity. Render the cloud from a known camera, compare it with the real photo, nudge every ellipsoid towards less difference. Eight thousand times.

It does nothing in the end. It is the room — and it works in that one room only.

The driving model

Real machine learning

Camera image in, a decision out: free or blocked, chair or table, steer left or right. Trained once, then run many times a second on the robot.

It generalises — it handles rooms it has never seen, and knows none of them in particular.

That is the whole difference. One knows a single room perfectly and nothing else. The other knows no room, but copes anywhere.

Open source we stand on

Six projects, and what each one is for

Brush

Trains the 3D model. Gaussian splatting on Apple Silicon through WGPU/Metal — the reason this runs without an NVIDIA card. Its live viewer is what makes the training watchable.

ArthurBrussee/brush · Apache-2.0 · 5.0k ★

COLMAP

Finds the camera. Recovers a pose per frame and undistorts the fisheye. Everything downstream inherits its mistakes.

colmap/colmap · BSD · 12.6k ★

SuperSplat

Ships the result. Compresses .ply to .sog and writes a self-contained viewer — the one embedded at the end of this deck.

playcanvas/supersplat · MIT · 9.9k ★

JetBot

Drives the robot. Motor control and camera, version 0.4.3, already on the delivered image. We add to it and change nothing.

NVIDIA-AI-IOT/jetbot · MIT · 3.3k ★

jetson-inference

Recognises objects on the robot with a pre-trained SSD-MobileNet. Placing the labels in 3D happens on the Mac, from the poses.

dusty-nv/jetson-inference · MIT · 9.0k ★ · image from the project README

nerf_bridge

Lent us two ideas, not code: reject a soft frame by Laplacian variance, and stop when consecutive frames stop changing — which is what a robot stuck against furniture produces.

javieryu/nerf_bridge · borrowed approach

The interface is deliberately dumb: a folder of numbered JPEGs, plus one file of detections per image. No protocol, no shared library. Each side stays runnable and repairable without the other — which is also why each side is its own demo when the other one breaks.

Driving, geometrically

Two obvious ways to drive produce unusable data

Spin on the spot

Plenty of overlap, no triangulation. The camera sits ahead of the wheel axis, so a spin traces a small circle rather than turning about the lens — an offset nobody has measured yet.

Drive at the wall

The expansion point sits at the image centre, where displacement is zero. The 160° fisheye still earns parallax at the edges, which is why wide lenses tolerate forward motion at all.

Pass the wall sideways

This is the one that works. A closed loop parallel to the walls, driven twice at different distances, so the same surface is seen from two depths.

The driving algorithm

Four ways to make the robot do the moving

Built, untested on hardware

Fixed manoeuvres

One key runs a whole sequence: orbit, wall pass, panorama. The human picks the standpoint, the robot performs the motion a keyboard is bad at. Any key aborts mid-run.

Decided as D-014 · verified against a stand-in robot only

Planned

Trained steering

A ResNet-18 regression head, labelled one click per frame, trained on the Nano itself. It replaces the weakest primitive — the long wall pass, where an open loop drifts.

Only if packages 1–3 show the drift is actually bad

Next

Boot the robot

First boot, join the Wi-Fi, reach Jupyter. Nobody can do this over SSH — it is a person in a room with a power switch.

Then

Run checkup.py

It answers the two questions that block everything: does the camera really deliver 1280 × 960, and does the SD card keep up? Every JetBot notebook builds the camera at 224 × 224.

Then

One real drive

A corner of a room, handed to the pipeline. That is the proof the two halves fit — and the first frames our own camera has ever produced.

Nothing in the pipeline half waits on any of this. That was the point of splitting them: the Mac side was built, measured and finished while the robot sat in its box.

Vision
Not built — this is where we want to go

A drive that publishes itself

Park the robot, and the run finishes on its own: fetch, poses, training, compression, upload. Some minutes later a link exists, and the room is on every phone in the room. No command in between.

Why it is not there yet

The Lenovo rollout is postponed on purpose. It changes the address the viewer lives at and nothing else, so it was the cheapest thing to defer.

The real constraint

Uploading 6.8 MB over ~9 Mbit/s takes about six seconds. The problem is the other direction: a whole lecture hall loading it at once.

So, for the demo

Serve it from the Mac, and treat the web link as the thing you take home afterwards.
